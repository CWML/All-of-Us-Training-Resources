Workflows for Beginners

What workflows are, why researchers use them in All of Us and elsewhere, when not to, and the ideas behind dsub, Cromwell, and Nextflow, in plain language

New to workflows? This page explains what they are, why researchers use them, and the ideas you’ll meet in the class Running Workflows. You don’t need to read it all at once, and you don’t need to know any of it to use the All of Us data: most projects never need a workflow.

The big picture: one oven, or a kitchen for hire

Imagine you have to bake 22 cakes, each from its own bag of ingredients.

Baking at home (your app) Hiring a kitchen (a workflow)
Ovens one, so one cake after another one per cake, all baking at once
Size your oven, whatever the cake a big oven for the big cakes, a small one for the small ones
You stay home until the last cake is done send the order, and come back when it’s ready
Paying for your kitchen, all day for each oven only while it bakes

In workflow words:

In the kitchen In a workflow In the class
the recipe the script: the commands to run gwas_one_chr.sh
the ingredients the inputs: files copied onto each machine the ClinVar files for one chromosome, and our sample
a kitchen kit with every tool already in it the image (container): the software, already installed PLINK 2
the ovens the machines (virtual machines in the cloud) 22 n2-standard-2 machines
the order ticket that lists every cake the tasks file, or the workflow file gwas_tasks.tsv
the head chef who hands out the work the workflow engine: dsub, Cromwell, or Nextflow dsub
the counter where finished cakes go your bucket data/workflows/
the kitchen’s notes on each cake the logs data/workflows/logs/

Why researchers use workflows

Reason What it means Example
⏱️ Speed many machines work in parallel 22 chromosomes in the time of the slowest one, instead of the sum of all 22
📈 Scale each task gets a machine as big as it needs, far bigger than a laptop or an app 32 CPUs and 400 GB of disk for one chromosome of the full genome data
🔁 Reproducibility the workflow file records every step, the software versions, and the settings. Rerun it next year and get the same results a reviewer asks you to rerun with a different filter: change one line, run again
🤝 Sharing a colleague runs your workflow on their data, or you run a published one the GATK pipelines for finding variants; the nf-core pipelines for RNA-seq
🛟 Recovery if one task fails, rerun only that task. Cromwell and Nextflow can resume a pipeline where it stopped a machine is stopped after 3 hours: only its task starts again
💤 Hands-off jobs run without you, even with your app stopped launch before you go home; results in the morning
💰 Cost control you pay for each machine only while it works, and can use cheap spot machines (see Is it cheaper? below)

Why in All of Us in particular

  • The data is big. All of Us has hundreds of thousands of whole genomes. One chromosome of the main genome callset (ACAF) is tens to hundreds of GB; in the All of Us tutorial, just copying one onto a machine took an hour. An app can’t hold the whole genome at once.
  • The data can’t leave. At most universities, big jobs go to the university’s computing cluster. All of Us data must stay in the Workbench, so you can’t take it to a cluster (at Yale: the Center for Research Computing’s clusters). Workflows are how you get a cluster’s power inside the Workbench.
  • Apps are one machine. A bigger app costs more every hour it’s on, including while you read, write, or wait. Workflows use big machines only for the minutes they’re needed.
  • Publishing. Journals and reviewers increasingly ask for reproducible code. A workflow file in GitHub, with pinned versions, is exactly that, and it contains no data.

When not to use a workflow

  • Exploring the data, trying ideas, making plots: use the app.
  • Small data, or steps that take minutes: starting machines and copying files would take longer than the work.
  • BigQuery work (most of the core, survey, and wearables classes): BigQuery already runs in parallel on Google’s machines. You don’t need a workflow for SQL.
  • Steps you haven’t tested yet: get them working in the app first.

👉 Rule of thumb: if you’d run the same step on many files (or one very big one), and it already works in your app, consider a workflow.

Is it cheaper?

Often faster; cheaper only sometimes. The total computing is about the same: 22 machines for 15 minutes cost about as much as 1 machine for 5½ hours.

A workflow saves money when… A workflow costs more when…
the alternative is a big app running for hours (including while you wait, or forget to stop it) the job is small: each machine takes a few minutes to start and copy its files, and that time is billed too
steps need different sizes: each task gets the machine it needs every task copies the same big files
you use spot machines: much cheaper, and the engine restarts a task if Google stops its machine a mistake runs in every task: 22 tasks, 22 times the cost. Test in the app first
you stop the app while the jobs run

In the class, the 22 tasks added up to about 2 hours of machine time (on SSD disks) but ran at the same time; the job cost well under $1.

Job machines and storage are billed to your workspace’s pod, like apps. They don’t appear in the app’s cost estimate, so estimate them yourself: machine price per hour × hours × number of tasks.

Common workflow patterns

Scatter–gather: split, run in parallel, combine

The most common pattern, and the one in the class: scatter one job into many tasks, then gather the results.

                ┌─► chr1  ─┐
one analysis ───┼─► chr2  ─┼───► one table of results
  (scatter)     ├─► ...   ─┤        (gather)
                └─► chr22 ─┘

You can split by chromosome or region (genomics), by sample or person (sequencing data, images), by batch of files, or by setting (the same model with different parameters).

A pipeline: steps in order

Each step uses the outputs of the one before. Each step can scatter too.

raw files ──► quality control ──► analysis ──► summary and plots

dsub runs one step per job, so you’d run (and check) each step yourself. Cromwell and Nextflow run the whole chain, in the right order, and start each step as soon as its inputs are ready.

What researchers run as workflows

Field Typical workflow
Genomics GWAS by chromosome (as in the class); finding variants in each person’s sequence (CRAM → VCF); annotating or filtering variant files; polygenic scores
RNA-seq from raw reads to counts, one task per sample (e.g. the nf-core rnaseq pipeline)
Imaging processing each scan the same way
Text running a language model over many documents
Wearables summarizing minute-level data, one batch of people per task
Statistics simulations, bootstraps, permutation tests: thousands of independent runs
Machine learning trying many model settings at once

The ideas behind them

Containers and images: the kitchen kit

An image is a packaged computer system with the software already installed: PLINK, R and its packages, Python, and so on. A container is a copy of the image, running. Every task runs inside one, so:

  • the software is the same on every machine, today and next year
  • you don’t install anything on the machines
  • others can rerun your work with the exact same software

Images live in registries (e.g. Docker Hub, Google’s Artifact Registry) and have tags, which name a version: plink2:alpha2.3_jan2020. Two rules:

  • Pin the version. A tag like latest changes when a new version comes out, and your results (or your job) can change with it. That’s what broke every dsub job in All of Us in November 2026 (see the class).
  • In All of Us, job machines can’t reach Docker Hub. Use images from Google’s registries, or Docker Hub images through the All of Us mirror.

You don’t need to build images to start: ready-made ones exist for most tools (e.g. the BioContainers project has images for most bioinformatics software).

Inputs and outputs: what the machine can see

Each machine starts empty. It sees only the files you list as inputs: the engine copies them in (localizing) before your script runs, and copies the outputs back to your bucket (delocalizing) afterwards. So:

  • every file the script uses must be an input (in the class: the .bim and .fam files too, though the script never names them)
  • copying takes time, and big files take a long time. Some engines can stream files instead of copying them (Nextflow in the Workbench can), which helps when a tool reads only part of a file
  • the disk matters. Cloud disks come in types: standard (cheap, slow, and slowest when small) and SSD (faster, a bit more per GB). In the class, chromosome 1 (a 27.7 GB file) took 101 minutes on dsub’s default standard disk, and 8 minutes on an SSD (--disk-type pd-ssd). Most of the difference was copying the file: about 8 MB a second onto the standard disk, about 130 onto the SSD

Parallelism and limits

Tasks that don’t depend on each other can run at the same time. But your project has limits (quotas) on how many machines and CPUs run at once. Above the limit, tasks wait in a queue: the job still finishes, just later. A task can also wait when Google has no free machine of the type you asked for: in the class, 21 of 22 tasks finished in under 8 minutes, and one waited 30 minutes for its machine. The job finishes when its slowest task does.

Logs: your first stop

Every task saves a log: the engine’s messages (copying files, starting the script) and everything your script printed. When a task fails, read its log before changing anything. When a task is slow, read its time stamps: they show whether the time went on copying files or on the analysis.

Retries, spot machines, and resuming

  • Retries: start a failed task again, automatically (--retries in dsub).
  • Spot machines (preemptible): much cheaper, but Google can stop them at any time. With retries, a stopped task starts again.
  • Resuming: Cromwell (call caching) and Nextflow (-resume) remember which steps finished, and skip them when you rerun the pipeline.

Reproducibility: write it down

Keep the workflow (script, tasks file, or workflow file) in GitHub, with the image versions pinned. Record the date and the data version (e.g. CDR v9). That’s your methods section, and it contains no data.

👉 Which should I learn? Start with dsub: it’s one command, and it teaches every idea on this page. Move to Nextflow or Cromwell when you need multi-step pipelines, or want to run a published one. Pick the one your field (or your collaborators) uses: much of genomics at the Broad Institute and on Terra uses WDL; nf-core uses Nextflow.

Outside the Workbench

The same ideas work everywhere, with your own data (not All of Us data):

  • University clusters (e.g. Yale’s Center for Research Computing) run jobs with a scheduler called Slurm. A Slurm job array (sbatch --array=1-22) is the same idea as dsub’s tasks file.
  • Nextflow and Snakemake (a Python-based workflow tool, popular in academia) run on laptops, clusters, and clouds with the same workflow file.
  • Terra runs WDL workflows on Google Cloud, for many large research programs.

So the skills from the class carry over to most research computing.

Workflows and the All of Us rules

Workflows don’t change the rules:

Rule In a workflow
Data stays in the Workbench Jobs run in your workspace’s cloud project and private network, and write to your bucket, so the data stays inside
Outputs are All of Us data Job results and logs are as sensitive as anything else you make from the data. Keep them in your bucket
No individual-level data in logs Don’t make scripts print rows of data, participant IDs, or dates: logs are saved, and are easy to copy
No counts of 1 to 20 when you share The same for workflow results: summarize first, then check
Code goes to GitHub, data doesn’t Scripts and workflow files: yes. Tasks files with participant IDs in them: no
Share notebooks through GitHub, with outputs cleared Never download a notebook to share it, even one that looks free of data: its outputs leave the Workbench that way, and downloads are monitored

Questions beginners ask

Do I need to know Docker? No. Use ready-made images (the class uses PLINK’s). Learn to build your own later, if you need software that isn’t in any image.

Can a job run R? Yes: use an image with R in it (e.g. one of the rocker images, through the All of Us mirror), and run Rscript my_script.R as the command. Any R packages the script needs must be in the image, or installed by the script.

Can I close my browser, or stop the app, while jobs run? Yes. The jobs run on their own machines, and their results go to your bucket. Check on them later with dstat, or on the Workflows tab for Cromwell and Nextflow. (dsub jobs don’t appear on that tab.)

How do I know what a job cost? There’s no per-job bill. Estimate it: machine price per hour × hours × tasks, plus disks. The class shows how to get each task’s run time.

What if one task out of 22 fails? Read its log, fix the cause, and rerun only that task (in dsub, with the task’s row number after the tasks file).

Can I run a published workflow? Yes. Find it on Dockstore or nf-core and add it on the Workflows tab. Check its images first: in All of Us, they must come from Google’s registries or the mirror.

Words you’ll meet

Word Means

Job, task one launch of a workflow; the separate pieces of work inside it (e.g. one per chromosome)

Image, container packaged software; a running copy of it
Registry, tag where images are kept; the name of one version
Mirror a copy of Docker Hub inside Google Cloud, for All of Us workspaces

Scatter, gather split work into parallel tasks; combine their results
Spot (preemptible) cheaper machines that Google can stop at any time
Standard disk, SSD a machine’s disk: cheap and slow, or faster and a bit dearer (--disk-type pd-ssd in dsub)

Google Batch the Google Cloud service the engines use to run machines

Call caching, resume reusing finished steps when a pipeline is rerun

A learning path

  1. Read this page, then do the class Running Workflows: one script, one job, then 22.
  2. Change the class’s script: a different filter, or a different PLINK command (e.g. allele frequencies of common variants). Test it in the app, then as a job.
  3. Learn a workflow language on your own computer, with public example data: the free Nextflow training, or the WDL documentation.
  4. Run a published workflow from Dockstore or nf-core on the Workflows tab, starting with its small test data.
