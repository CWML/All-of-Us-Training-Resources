Machine Learning for Beginners

The ideas behind the machine learning series, in plain language: prediction vs explanation, training and test data, overfitting, AUC, calibration, and leakage

New to machine learning? This page explains everything the machine learning series assumes, in plain language, with examples from our type 2 diabetes question. Each class links back to the parts it uses.

What is machine learning?

Machine learning is a set of methods that learn a rule from examples, so the rule can be used on new cases. Show a method thousands of people, what was known about them, and whether they were later diagnosed with type 2 diabetes. It learns a rule that turns “what’s known” into a predicted risk. Then we use the rule on people it has never seen.

You’ve already used one machine learning method: logistic regression (class 3.2). The difference is in why we use it.

Explaining vs predicting

Explaining (classes 3.2, 5.3, 6.2) Predicting (this series)
The question Is income linked to type 2 diabetes, after accounting for age and sex? For this person, what’s the chance of a diagnosis in the next 2 years?
What matters one effect (an odds ratio or hazard ratio), and its confidence interval how accurate the predicted risks are, for new people
Judged by is the effect unbiased (confounders adjusted for)? AUC and calibration on a test set
A predictor that causes nothing a problem (confounding) fine, if it’s known in time and it helps
The main worries confounding, reverse causation overfitting, leakage

A predictor can be useful without being a cause. People who rate their health as poor are more often diagnosed with diabetes soon after, perhaps because they already have it, undiagnosed. That answer helps a prediction, but changing someone’s answer wouldn’t change their risk.

Four parts of a prediction question

Every class in this series starts by filling in this table:

Part Our choice in 7.1 Why it matters
Who is it for? adults with no diabetes at enrollment you can’t predict a diagnosis that has already happened
From what? (known when?) age, sex, BMI, blood pressure, and survey answers, from around enrollment a model used on a given day can only use what’s known that day
Predicting what? a new type 2 diabetes diagnosis the shared case definition, with its date
By when? within 2 years the prediction window: longer windows give more cases, but fewer people with long enough follow-up

The day the prediction is made is time zero. Everything used as a predictor must be known by then; the outcome must happen after it.

Why time zero matters in All of Us

Most people with type 2 diabetes in All of Us were diagnosed before they joined. In our ML data check, about 77% of cases were diagnosed before enrollment. Their surveys and measurements describe life with diabetes, so they can’t teach a model the warning signs. They’re left out, and the model learns from the people who were diagnosed afterwards:

Data People at risk at time zero New type 2 diabetes afterwards
Surveys and BMI at enrollment about 264,000 about 15,500
Fitbit (from the first day of data) about 34,000 about 2,000
Proteomics (from the blood draw) about 5,800 about 380

That last row is why the protein class (7.3) has to be careful: thousands of proteins, and a few hundred cases.

Leakage: the most common way to fool yourself

Leakage is when a predictor contains information you wouldn’t have at prediction time, often because it’s a result of the outcome. A leaky model looks brilliant in the analysis and fails in practice.

Predictor Leakage? Why
BMI measured at enrollment ✅ no known at time zero
An HbA1c test result after enrollment ❌ yes HbA1c is how diabetes is diagnosed
A metformin prescription ❌ yes a treatment for diabetes
A survey answered after the diagnosis ❌ yes the answer may reflect the diagnosis (class 5.2)
The number of doctor visits in the next year ❌ yes it’s in the future, and people being diagnosed see doctors more
Being in the “case” group of our cohort ❌ yes that is the outcome

Rule of thumb: an AUC above about 0.95 for a common disease, from routine data, is almost always leakage. Check every predictor’s date.

Training and test data

A model always looks better on the people it learned from: it has partly memorised them. To know how it would do on new people, we hide some people from it.

everyone in the analysis
├── training set (75%): the model learns from these
└── test set (25%):     locked away; used ONCE, at the very end
Word Means
Training set the people a model learns from
Test set people held back, to measure how the model does on people it never saw
Stratified split both sets get the same share of cases (important when the outcome is rare)
Seed (set.seed()) makes a random split the same every time, and for everyone in the class

Overfitting

Overfitting is learning the noise in the training set, not the pattern. An overfitted model scores very well on training data and worse on new data. It’s more likely when there are many predictors and few cases.

Too simple (underfit) About right Too flexible (overfit)
Training data so-so good excellent
Test data so-so good worse
Example age alone age, sex, BMI, … a separate rule for every combination of answers

Methods such as LASSO (class 7.1) fight overfitting with a penalty that keeps the model simple.

Cross-validation and tuning

Many methods have settings you choose before fitting, called tuning parameters: LASSO’s penalty, or the number of trees in a forest (7.2). We can’t use the test set to choose them: the test set would then be part of the training, and its result no longer honest.

Cross-validation chooses them using the training set only:

training set, cut into 5 folds:   [1][2][3][4][5]
round 1: learn from 2-5, score on 1
round 2: learn from 1, 3-5, score on 2
...                                   every person is scored once, by a model that didn't see them

Try each setting this way, keep the best, then fit once on the whole training set, and score once on the test set.

Measuring a model

AUC: does it rank people well?

The AUC (area under the ROC curve) is the chance that a randomly chosen person who was diagnosed got a higher predicted risk than a randomly chosen person who wasn’t.

AUC Means
0.5 no better than a coin toss
0.7 acceptable; typical of models built from routine data
0.8 good
0.9 excellent: rare for a common disease
1.0 perfect: suspect leakage

Why not accuracy? If 5% of people are diagnosed, a model that says “no” to everyone is 95% accurate, and useless. The AUC isn’t fooled by a rare outcome.

The ROC curve: what does a cut-off cost?

To act on a risk (offer a test, say), you choose a cut-off: everyone above it is flagged. Each cut-off trades one kind of mistake for another:

Word Means In our example
Sensitivity the share of future cases that are flagged of those diagnosed within 2 years, how many we’d have tested
Specificity the share of everyone else that isn’t flagged of those not diagnosed, how many we left alone
Positive predictive value the share of flagged people who are diagnosed of those we tested, how many went on to a diagnosis

The ROC curve draws sensitivity against 1 − specificity for every cut-off. The AUC is the area under it.

Calibration: are the numbers right?

The AUC only checks the order of the risks. A model can rank people perfectly and still say “10%” when the truth is “2%”. If a doctor tells a patient “your risk is 8%”, the 8% must be right.

Calibration checks this: group people by predicted risk (for example, into tenths), and compare each group’s average predicted risk with the share actually diagnosed. On a calibration plot, a well-calibrated model’s points lie on the diagonal.

Good AUC Poor AUC
Good calibration ranks well, numbers right: useful numbers right on average, but can’t tell people apart
Poor calibration ranks well, numbers wrong: fine for “who’s highest?”, not for “what’s my risk?” not useful

Models often lose calibration when used in a different population (another hospital, another country), even when their AUC holds up.

The methods in this series

Method Idea Class
Logistic regression a weighted sum of predictors, turned into a risk 7.1 (and 3.2)
LASSO logistic regression with a penalty that shrinks weak predictors to exactly zero: it chooses predictors 7.1, 7.3
Splines let a number’s effect bend (risk may rise faster at high BMI) 7.1
Random forest many decision trees, each on a random sample, voting together 7.2
Gradient boosting trees built one after another, each fixing the last one’s mistakes 7.2

A decision tree is a flowchart of yes/no questions (BMI over 30? Age over 55?) ending in a risk. Trees find interactions by themselves, but a single tree overfits easily; forests and boosting combine many.

We don’t use deep learning (neural networks). It shines with images, text, and signals, and needs very large data. On tables of a few dozen predictors, it rarely beats the methods above.

Tools: tidymodels

The R classes use tidymodels, a set of packages that share one way of working, in the style of the tidyverse:

Package Does Example

recipes preparing predictors step_dummy(), step_normalize()
parsnip one way to specify many models logistic_reg(), rand_forest()
workflows a recipe and a model, together workflow()
tune, dials trying settings tune_grid(), penalty()
yardstick measuring performance roc_auc()

Python users: the same ideas are in scikit-learn (7.2 shows one model in both).

Prediction, fairness, and precision medicine

Prediction models are one route from group results to individual care: precision medicine, the reason All of Us exists. They also bring new risks:

  • A model can work worse for some groups, especially groups it saw few of. Check its AUC and calibration by group (7.4).
  • Predictors can carry unfairness. Income predicts diabetes. Should a clinical model use it? Leaving it out doesn’t remove the inequality; using it may label people by their circumstances.
  • The outcome is a diagnosis, not the disease. People who see doctors more are diagnosed sooner. A model can learn who gets diagnosed, not only who gets diabetes.
  • Reporting: the TRIPOD+AI guideline lists what a prediction model paper should report (7.4).

Glossary

Word Means
Feature machine learning’s word for a predictor
Label machine learning’s word for the outcome
Time zero the moment a prediction is made: predictors before it, outcome after it
Prediction window (horizon) how far ahead we predict, e.g. 2 years
Incident case a new diagnosis after time zero (vs prevalent: already diagnosed)
Leakage a predictor that contains information from after time zero, or from the outcome itself
Overfitting learning the training set’s noise; good on training data, worse on new data
Tuning parameter a setting chosen before fitting, e.g. the LASSO penalty
Cross-validation choosing settings by fitting on part of the training set and scoring on the rest, in turn
Penalty (regularisation) a cost on large coefficients that keeps a model simple
AUC the chance a future case is ranked above a non-case; 0.5 = coin toss
Calibration whether predicted risks match what happened
External validation testing a model on a different population from the one it was built on
