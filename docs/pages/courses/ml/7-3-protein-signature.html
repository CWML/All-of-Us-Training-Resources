7.3 A Protein Signature

Thousands of blood proteins, a few hundred new cases: a LASSO panel, nested cross-validation, and the real question: do proteins add anything beyond age, sex, and BMI?

Machine learning series, class 3 of 4 · R · 95 minutes

Open the notebook on GitHub Machine learning series overview

Overview

In 7.1 and 7.2, a handful of enrollment measures predicted new type 2 diabetes about as well as any model could. Blood proteins are a different kind of data: about 5,000 measurements per person, for a few thousand people. Can a panel of proteins see diabetes coming before the routine measures do?

Why this class matters

Omics studies often report a “signature” that predicts disease. Many don’t hold up in new people. This class shows the three things that decide whether a signature is real: a time zero before the outcome, an honest test (nested cross-validation), and a comparison with what’s already known.

By the end you’ll be able to

  1. Set time zero for a blood sample, and keep the outcome strictly after it
  2. Explain why ordinary logistic regression fails with more predictors than cases, and how LASSO copes
  3. Use nested cross-validation when there are too few cases for a separate test set
  4. Ask whether new data adds anything beyond age, sex, and BMI, with a bootstrap confidence interval
  5. Check whether the proteins LASSO picks are stable

Key concepts

More predictors than cases

7.1 7.3
Predictors 8 about 5,000 proteins
New cases about 8,500 a few hundred
Cases per predictor over 1,000 less than 1

With more predictors than cases, ordinary logistic regression can always find a combination that separates the training cases perfectly: it memorizes the people, and fails on anyone new. LASSO pulls every protein’s coefficient towards zero and sets most to exactly zero; the proteins left over are the panel.

Time zero for a blood sample

  enrollment       blood draw = time zero                       2 years later
      |                    |──────────────── follow-up ─────────────────|
  age, sex, BMI      proteins measured        a NEW type 2 diabetes diagnosis? yes / no
                     excluded: anyone diagnosed by now

Time zero is the later of enrollment and the blood draw: the first moment we’d have both. Proteins change with diabetes, so a sample taken after diagnosis would make a panel look excellent, and be useless for prediction.

Nested cross-validation

A 25% test set would hold only about 50 new cases: its AUC could be off by 0.05 or more. Instead, every person is tested once:

Outer loop: split everyone into 5 folds
  For each fold k:
    Inner loop: cross-validation on the other 4 folds, to choose the LASSO penalty
    Fit on the other 4 folds; predict fold k        <- people the model never saw

Two loops, because two things need unseen data: choosing the penalty (inner), and measuring the model (outer).

Does it add anything?

Model Predictors Fitted with
Clinical age, sex at birth, BMI logistic regression
Proteins every protein LASSO
Clinical + proteins age, sex, BMI, and every protein LASSO, with age, sex, and BMI never penalized: proteins must earn their place on top

The bootstrap (resample the people 1,000 times and recompute) gives a confidence interval for the difference in AUC.

Code walk-through

One fold of nested cross-validation

run_fold <- function(k) {
  train <- outer_fold != k
  test  <- outer_fold == k
1  prot  <- standardize(proteins[train, ], proteins[test, ])
  clin  <- standardize(clinical[train, ], clinical[test, ])

  both_fit <- cv.glmnet(cbind(clin$train, prot$train), y[train], family = "binomial",
2                        foldid = inner_folds[[k]], standardize = FALSE,
3                        penalty.factor = c(0, 0, 0, rep(1, ncol(prot$train))))
  predict(both_fit, cbind(clin$test, prot$test), s = "lambda.min", type = "response")
}

4folds <- parallel::mclapply(1:5, run_fold, mc.cores = min(5, CORES))
1
Means and SDs from the training folds only; a missing value becomes the training average. Using everyone’s would leak the test people into the training.
2
cv.glmnet() is the inner loop. foldid fixes its folds, so the results are the same every run.
3
0: age, sex, and BMI are never penalized; 1: every protein is.
4
mclapply() is map() on several processors: the 5 folds run at once.

A confidence interval for a difference

boot <- map(1:1000, function(b) {
1  i <- sample(nrow(predictions), replace = TRUE)
  d <- predictions[i, ]
  tibble(clinical = auc(d$y, d$clinical), both = auc(d$y, d$both))
}) %>% list_rbind()

2quantile(boot$both - boot$clinical, c(0.025, 0.975))
1
The same resampled people for both models: the difference is measured more precisely than either AUC.
2
The middle 95% of 1,000 differences: the 95% confidence interval.

What we found

From our test run in the Workbench (November 2026).

The people

Of 152,586 people in 7.1’s file, 3,886 had proteomics, no type 2 diabetes by time zero, and 2 years of follow-up (or a diagnosis sooner). 150 developed type 2 diabetes within 2 years (3.86%): 0.03 cases per protein. For 62% of them, time zero was the enrollment day; for the rest, the blood was drawn later. All 5,420 proteins passed the missing-value filter.

Proteins add real information

Model Cross-validated AUC 95% CI Brier score
Clinical: age, sex, BMI 0.697 0.656 to 0.740 0.0366
Proteins only (LASSO) 0.750 0.707 to 0.791 0.0357
Clinical + proteins (LASSO) 0.759 0.720 to 0.793 0.0357
Everyone gets the average risk 0.500 0.0371
  • Proteins on their own ranked people better than age, sex, and BMI.
  • The gain from adding proteins: 0.062 (95% CI 0.032 to 0.089), above 0 in every one of 1,000 bootstrap resamples. That’s about 8 times the gain boosting gave in 7.2.
  • Each AUC is uncertain (about ±0.04, with 150 cases), but the difference is measured more precisely, because the same people are in both models.
  • The Brier scores barely move: with a rare outcome, every model’s risks are small, and so are its errors.
  • Calibration: both models are close where counts can be shown (top fifth with proteins: 9.45% predicted, 9.91% diagnosed). The protein model spreads people further apart, from about 1% to 9.5% predicted.

The panel is unstable, as expected

Nested cross-validation ran in 2 minutes. The same method, on four-fifths of the same people, kept 21 to 64 proteins. The final model kept 98:

5 5
4 6
3 12
2 15
1 34

  • Only 5 were chosen every time: ENTPD5, UMOD, FAM114A1, ADGRG1, and TFF1. The model as a whole predicts well; the exact list is not reliable.
  • Not BMI in disguise: the top 20 barely correlate with BMI (the strongest, SELE, at 0.27).
  • Small effects, combined: odds ratios of 0.83 to 1.13 per SD. The panel works by adding up many weak signals.
  • Echoes of 4.6, which compared people who already had diabetes: UMOD (made by the kidney) lower, LGALS4 and ADGRG1 higher. Here they appear before diagnosis.

The lesson: a new kind of data can add real predictive information (here, more than any modelling trick in 7.2), but with few cases, which proteins carry it can’t be pinned down. Before such a panel could be used, it would need testing in other people: we don’t know how people were chosen for proteomics, and 150 cases is a small base.

Instructor notes

  • Draw the two loops of nested cross-validation. Ask: what goes wrong if we choose the penalty on all the data, then cross-validate? (The test folds helped choose it: optimistic.)
  • Start the long cells before discussing: Part 3 (5 to 8 minutes) and Part 5 (5 to 20).
  • Expect an unstable panel: with a few hundred cases, many proteins are chosen in only one or two folds. That’s the honest finding, and one reason published panels often fail to replicate.
  • Don’t publish two versions of these counts (for example, with enrollment and with the blood draw as time zero): their differences would be small counts.

Key takeaways

  • With more predictors than cases, ordinary regression memorizes; LASSO keeps a small panel.
  • When cases are few, nested cross-validation uses everyone for testing, with an inner loop for tuning.
  • Ask what new data adds to cheap predictors, on the same people, with a confidence interval for the difference.
  • Check how stable a panel is before trusting it.
  • Time zero must come before the outcome: for blood markers, the day the blood was drawn.
