3.2 Logistic Regression

Odds and odds ratios, univariable and multivariable models, readable scales, comparing models on the same people, and reverse causation

Core series, class 7 of 9 · R · 85 minutes

Open the notebook on GitHub Core series overview

Overview

Table 1 showed that cases are older and heavier, with different blood pressure and cholesterol. But those differences overlap: older people are also heavier, on average. Logistic regression asks how each characteristic goes with the odds of type 2 diabetes while holding the others constant.

Why this class matters

Logistic regression is the most common model in health research for yes/no outcomes, and the same model powers the GWAS in 4.2. This class also shows two of its classic traps in real data: adjusting for a variable that defines the outcome (HbA1c), and reverse causation (LDL cholesterol after treatment).

By the end you’ll be able to

  1. Explain odds and odds ratios
  2. Fit univariable and multivariable models with glm(), and read them with broom::tidy()
  3. Put predictors on readable scales
  4. Compare models fitted on the same people
  5. Explain why HbA1c is left out, and what reverse causation is

Analysis at a glance

❓ Question Which characteristics go with higher odds of type 2 diabetes, accounting for each other?
🧪 The analysis Logistic regression: type 2 diabetes ~ age + sex + BMI + blood pressure + LDL, built up step by step
🎯 Why A yes/no outcome; multivariable, because the predictors are related (confounding)
✅ What we conclude Adjusted odds ratios, e.g. per 5 BMI units, at the same age and sex
⚠️ What it can’t tell us Cause. Measurements taken after diagnosis can reflect the disease or its treatment
🔗 Connections The same model as the GWAS in 4.2; 3.3 is the version for a numeric outcome

Key concepts

  • Odds = chance it happens ÷ chance it doesn’t (20% → 0.25).
  • Odds ratio (OR): the odds in one group ÷ another. 1 = no difference; 2 = twice the odds.
  • For a number like BMI, the OR is per unit, so we choose a readable unit: per 5 BMI units, per 10 years.
  • Our sample is half cases by design (a case-control sample). That makes risk meaningless here, but odds ratios stay valid: that’s why case-control studies report them.
Univariable Multivariable
Model diabetes ~ one predictor diabetes ~ several
OR is crude adjusted for the others

Try It Out

Tip✋ Try It Out · 10 minutes · Part 5, “Your turn”

Requiring LDL dropped many people. Refit age + sex + BMI on everyone with those three variables. How many people does it use, and does the BMI OR change?

✅ Check: many more people (especially controls), and a similar BMI OR: a sensitivity analysis.

Code walk-through

1model_data <- fa %>% drop_na(t2d_binary, age10, sex_at_birth, BMI5, SBP10, LDL10)

models <- list(
2  "1. Age + sex"             = glm(t2d_binary ~ age10 + sex_at_birth,  data = model_data, family = binomial),
  "2. + BMI"                 = glm(t2d_binary ~ age10 + sex_at_birth + BMI5, data = model_data, family = binomial),
  "3. + blood pressure, LDL" = glm(t2d_binary ~ age10 + sex_at_birth + BMI5 + SBP10 + LDL10,
                                   data = model_data, family = binomial)
)

3imap(models, ~ tidy(.x, exponentiate = TRUE, conf.int = TRUE) %>% mutate(model = .y))
1
One data set with every model variable present, so all three models use the same people. Otherwise a change in an OR could just be a change in who’s in the model.
2
glm(…, family = binomial) is logistic regression. age10, BMI5, … are the predictors divided by 10 or 5, for readable ORs.
3
tidy(exponentiate = TRUE) turns coefficients into odds ratios, with 95% confidence intervals; imap() labels each model’s rows.

What we found

Results from our test run in October 2026 (CDR v9).

Crude OR Adjusted OR (model 3)
Age, per 10 years 1.43 1.41
Male (vs female) 1.25 1.14
BMI, per 5 units 1.46 1.59
Systolic BP, per 10 mm Hg 1.15 1.09
LDL, per 10 mg/dL 0.87 0.88
  • The multivariable models used 6,397 people out of 9,905: mostly controls without an LDL result.
  • BMI got stronger after adjusting for age: younger people are heavier on average but have less diabetes, which hid part of BMI’s link. Adjustment can move an OR either way.
  • LDL’s OR is below 1, which looks protective. It isn’t: most people with diabetes take statins, and our LDL is each person’s latest value, usually measured after treatment began. That’s reverse causation: diabetes affects LDL, not the other way round.
  • AIC fell from 8,400 to 7,761 to 7,512: each step improved the fit, BMI most of all.

💬 Discuss: which other predictors could be affected by having diabetes or its treatment? (Weight, with some diabetes medicines; blood pressure, often treated too.) What would fix it? (Measurements from before diagnosis.)

Instructor notes

TipInstructor tip
  • Ask students to guess LDL’s OR before running the models. Most expect it above 1.
  • HbA1c is left out on purpose: it defines diabetes. If a student adds it, the model warns about fitted probabilities of 0 or 1: a nice teaching moment.

Key takeaways

  • Logistic regression is for yes/no outcomes; exp(coefficient) is the odds ratio
  • Compare models on the same people
  • Put predictors on readable scales
  • Don’t adjust for what defines the outcome
  • Measurements taken after diagnosis can show reverse causation
