Precision Medicine: Why All of Us Exists, and Where Our Classes Fit

What precision medicine means, why the All of Us Research Program was built for it, how each course series connects to the program’s goals, and what our results can and can’t say about individual people

All of Us was created for precision medicine. This page explains what that means, and how each series in this course connects to it. It’s a good starting point for any class, and a good closing discussion for every series.

What is precision medicine?

Most medical advice is based on the average person in a study: the same screening age, the same first drug, the same advice for everyone. Precision medicine tailors prevention, diagnosis, and treatment to the individual, by taking into account how people differ in their genes, environment, and way of life.

The program’s founding paper describes its purpose like this:

“The All of Us data repository should permit researchers to take into account individual differences in lifestyle, socioeconomic factors, environment, and biologic characteristics in order to advance precision diagnosis, prevention, and treatment.” — The All of Us Research Program Investigators, New England Journal of Medicine, 2019 (doi:10.1056/NEJMsr1809937)

The program began in 2015 as the Precision Medicine Initiative cohort program. You’ll still see the old name, PMI, in the survey data (class 5.1).

Why precision medicine needed a program like All of Us

The founding paper names three problems with earlier studies: they were too small, they lacked diversity, and they didn’t collect enough kinds of data about each person. All of Us was designed to fix all three:

Earlier studies All of Us Why it matters for precision medicine
Thousands to hundreds of thousands of people A goal of 1 million or more Differences between people are often small; finding them needs very large numbers
Mostly people of European ancestry A focus on groups underrepresented in biomedical research: by 2019, 80% of participants with biospecimens were from these groups A treatment or risk score built on one group may not work as well for others
One or two kinds of data Health records, surveys, physical measurements, genomes, RNA and proteins, and wearables, for the same people “Genes, environment, and lifestyle” can only be studied together if they’re measured on the same people
Data downloaded project by project One cloud workbench, open to any approved researcher More researchers, and more questions, from the same data

How our series connect to the program’s goals

The founding paper lists the program’s scientific goals. Our series, all on the same type 2 diabetes question, touch most of them:

Program goal (from the founding paper) Where you practise it What it looks like in our classes
Discover genetic and environmental correlates with disease Omics 4.1–4.4; Surveys 5.2–5.3 A genetic risk variant (rs7903146 in TCF7L2); family history, smoking, income, education, and food security
Discover disease biomarkers Omics 4.5–4.7 Genes and blood proteins that differ in type 2 diabetes, and which of them are genetically controlled
Connect mobile health, digital health, and sensor data with clinical outcomes Wearables 6.1–6.3 Daily steps, sleep, and heart rate measured before a diagnosis, then followed forward to new type 2 diabetes
Improve understanding of health disparities Surveys 5.3; Core 3.1–3.2; Omics 4.1–4.2 Diabetes by income, education, and insurance; differences between groups in Table One; genetic ancestry
Enable machine-learning applications Machine learning 7.1–7.4 Predicting who will develop type 2 diabetes, from data collected before diagnosis
Develop new disease classifications; improve predictions of therapeutic safety and efficacy; support clinical trials Not covered yet Ideas for future classes, e.g. medication response (pharmacogenomics)

The Core series underpins all of them: finding the right people (cohorts), cleaning and joining their data, and the models every later class uses.

What our results can, and can’t, say about one person

Precision medicine is about individuals. Almost every result in our classes is about groups. Keeping the two apart is the most important lesson of this page.

Our classes find… Which means… It does not mean…
An odds ratio of about 1.25 per copy of rs7903146’s T allele (class 4.2) On average, each copy goes with about 25% higher odds of type 2 diabetes That a person with the allele will get diabetes, or one without it won’t. One common variant changes an individual’s risk only a little
People with type 2 diabetes more often had low household incomes (class 5.3) A pattern across groups That income causes diabetes, or that it tells you about a single person
People who walked more had lower rates of new diabetes (class 6.2) An association, in a design that reduces reverse causation That walking more will prevent diabetes for you: that needs a trial

The founding paper is candid about these limits too:

  • “Association does not necessarily translate into causation.” It points to methods such as Mendelian randomization and polygenic risk scores as ways to learn more, and to clinical trials to test it.
  • The sample isn’t random. The program “was not designed with a formal statistical sampling method”, so some results don’t describe the US population (class 5.1: no survey weights).
  • Health records are messy. EHR data “can be fragmented, incomplete, and inaccurate” (class 2.1, and the case definition in the shared cohort query).

From group results to individual care

Getting from our kind of result to precision medicine takes several steps, and each one is an active research area:

  1. Combine many factors. One variant, or one survey answer, predicts little. Polygenic risk scores add up thousands of variants; prediction models combine genes, records, surveys, and wearables (our machine learning series).
  2. Check it works for everyone. Today’s polygenic risk scores are several times more accurate in people of European ancestry than in other groups, because most genetic studies so far were in people of European ancestry. Using them in clinics could widen health disparities (Martin et al., Nature Genetics, 2019, doi:10.1038/s41588-019-0379-x). This is exactly the gap All of Us’s diversity is meant to close.
  3. Show cause, not just association, with designs such as Mendelian randomization and, finally, clinical trials.
  4. Show it helps patients: that tailoring care on the new information leads to better health than usual care.

💬 Discussion questions

Use these at the end of any series.

  • Which kind of data in this series (genes, records, surveys, wearables) would you most want for your risk of type 2 diabetes? Why?
  • Who is missing? Which groups are still underrepresented in our cohort (for example, people with no health records, or no smartphone for a wearable)? How could that affect a prediction made for them?
  • Group vs individual: pick one result from the series. What would you need to know before telling a patient it applies to them?
  • Harm: how could a precision medicine tool built on these data make care less fair? (The Research Use Statement asks about this too: see Research Use Statement.)
